# Chapter 14. What are you being built for?

## The question you have been carrying

In Chapter 1 I asked you to write down what you thought the next technology changing business was, and to keep the answer. Go and find it now, before you read the rest of this.

You were asked to name a capability, the arrangement it might change and a reason your prediction might fail. Read what you actually wrote. Do not quietly replace it with what you now wish you had written.

Perhaps you chose artificial intelligence. Perhaps you chose something less visible. The name matters less than the explanation. “This will be important” leaves almost everything open. Important to whom? Through what change in the work? Who benefits, who pays and what could prevent the result?

Make a brief record before continuing. Keep the original claim intact, distinguish evidence from your interpretation and put a real date beside the next review. If you have no new evidence, say so. If you did not keep an earlier answer, write a claim now and label it as a new starting point.

This last chapter turns the same questions towards the work you are preparing to do. It cannot promise what your first job will look like. It can help you decide what to practise before you get there.

## The handoff, one more time

Chapter 4 examined how shared information and AI might reduce routine transfers while preserving specialist work. There is a personal question inside that organisational one: if a tool can prepare the first draft, what should the person receiving it contribute?

Imagine an entry-level employee asked to summarise customer complaints. A tool produces the summary quickly. One possible response is to forward it. Another is to check whether repeated messages concern the same unresolved case, separate a delivery problem from a refund problem and identify the decision a manager could make.

The second person has done more than improve the wording. They have connected the records to the work and the work to a consequence.

This is a hypothetical situation, not a prediction that every graduate role will take that form. Some organisations may reduce junior drafting tasks. Others may use the released time to investigate more cases, improve service or train employees in different work. Some may struggle to obtain dependable results at all. The outcome depends on what the organisation chooses and whether the new arrangement works.

There is still a serious learning question. If an employer removes tasks through which beginners learned the business, where will that learning happen instead? Ask about supervised practice, access to experienced colleagues and feedback on decisions. Do not assume that faster production supplies those things automatically.

## What to practise when the future is uncertain

You do not need a confident forecast of graduate employment to identify useful work to practise. The book has repeatedly asked you to make a claim, inspect its evidence and connect it to a decision. Those are things you can do now.

Start with judgement under incomplete information. That does not mean trusting an instinct and calling it experience. It means stating what you know, what remains uncertain, which choice you recommend and what would make you reconsider. Chapter 10's incident exercise required that discipline before the cause was settled.

Then accountability. Being answerable involves following a decision into its consequences. Chapter 12's review was unfinished until a changed outcome reached the person affected. A recommendation is similarly unfinished if nobody knows who will act or check whether it helped.

Practise asking useful questions. What was counted and what was left out? Does this step serve a purpose we still need? What would make the proposal fail? Who would have to work differently? A good question makes the decision clearer; it does not merely make the questioner sound demanding.

There is a discipline in knowing when to stop asking, too. A decision may be due before every uncertainty can be resolved. Identify the missing evidence that could actually change the choice, seek it where practical and explain what risk remains. Do not use a request for more analysis to avoid a recommendation you already have enough information to make. If a small, reversible next step can supply useful evidence, say what it is and when you would review it.

Finally, practise connecting different kinds of evidence. A technically sound service may be too expensive for the business. An affordable one may depend on information it should not use. A useful one may leave employees with an unworkable process. Business and technical specialists both contribute to understanding those connections. Neither degree supplies the complete answer by itself.

These abilities are not beyond assessment. Can another person follow your calculation? Have you represented the strongest alternative fairly? Does your recommendation name a consequence, an owner and a limit? A reader can examine those things even when the eventual outcome is still unknown.

## Get close to a decision

Practical things, because a chapter of this kind that offers only themes is not enough.

Choose one decision you can observe appropriately: a campus organisation selecting a booking service, a workplace changing a routine or a course case involving a purchase. You do not need access to confidential meetings. A public explanation or a conversation with permission can supply a starting point.

Ask what alternatives were considered and what mattered most. Was the choice constrained by money, timing, staffing or a rule that could not be changed? What would the decision-maker have wanted to know earlier? Do not assume a choice you dislike was made without thought; find out what the person was trying to accomplish.

Write a short account of the decision in your own words. Separate the decision-maker's explanation from your inference. If the reason is unknown, leave it unknown. An invented explanation cannot teach you how somebody actually reasoned.

Next, choose one claim in the account to verify. It might concern the number of users, the time a task takes or what a quoted price includes. Follow it to the available record. Check the unit, period and definition. If the source does not answer the question, describe the gap rather than filling it with a plausible number.

Finally, produce one small piece of work you can defend: a process comparison, a cost estimate or a recommendation. Ask someone to challenge it. Listen for the assumption they cannot follow or the exception you omitted. Revise the work and preserve the earlier version so you can see what improved.

That is a learning plan with three connected parts: observe a decision, verify a claim and defend a piece of work. Set a date to complete it and identify the person from whom you will seek feedback. Keep the scope small enough to finish.

## Use a tool without borrowing its confidence

Use available tools where they help and where their use is permitted. The important test is whether you can explain and support the result you submit.

Return to the fictional complaint summary. Suppose the tool reports that delivery complaints doubled. Before recommending a change in the delivery service, ask what “complaint” counts: messages, customers, orders or unresolved cases. Check whether the two periods contain comparable volumes of orders. Read examples that support the summary and examples that might contradict it.

The tool may have helped you find a pattern. Your work is to establish what the pattern means well enough for the proposed decision. If you cannot explain why a number is what it is, you have a claim somebody else made.

Verification should fit the consequence. A private outline and a recommendation to spend company money do not require identical review. Identify the claims on which the recommendation depends and check those carefully. For a calculation, inspect the inputs and reproduce the result. For a factual assertion, examine the source. For an interpretation, consider an alternative explanation.

Keep the uncertainty visible when handing over the work. “This estimate assumes the same order volume in both periods” gives a manager something to examine. Removing that qualification to make the presentation sound cleaner makes the recommendation less useful.

Do not put confidential information into a tool simply because it is convenient. Follow the organisation's rules and use fictional or appropriately approved material for practice. The privacy questions in Chapter 11 apply to your own working habits too.

## Build the part that accumulates

Chapter 5 asked what an organisation has to maintain before it can respond well. There is a smaller version of that question for you.

Choose an area of business you want to understand beyond its vocabulary. Learn how work moves through it, which records matter, where people wait and what an error costs. Follow one transaction far enough to see why another department cares about it. Then ask what changes when the normal case becomes an exception.

You can begin without becoming an expert in an entire industry. Understanding how one order becomes a delivery and an invoice is a concrete start. So is understanding why a seemingly simple correction needs agreement from more than one person.

Keep a few pieces of work that show your reasoning developing. A useful record includes the original question, the evidence you used, a correction made after feedback and the resulting recommendation. Remove confidential details and obtain permission where needed. The point is to preserve what you learned, not to collect someone else's business records.

Reliability is also a habit you can practise. Say when you do not know, deliver what you agreed to deliver and correct mistakes openly. These actions give colleagues reasons to examine and trust your work. They do not guarantee a particular career outcome, but they are within your control in a way that the next labour-market forecast is not.

## What this book was arguing

Fourteen chapters, one argument: the arrangements are the subject.

Technology changes what people can do together, but a capability does not determine a business outcome on its own. People decide how work is organised, what the records mean, who has authority and what counts as success. Equipment and software make some arrangements possible; they do not make every arrangement worthwhile.

Improvement can come from removing a needless step or from doing a necessary one better. The question is what the step is for and what changes when you alter it. Better service can create value without creating an advantage competitors cannot copy.

Forecasts need the same separation. A growing capability, a growing market and a profitable investment are different claims. A convincing direction does not settle who will pay enough, for long enough, to justify a commitment.

A number needs a definition, a measurement and a purpose. An inference needs to be distinguished from an observed fact. A flag needs to be distinguished from a finding. These are small distinctions with large consequences when records guide decisions about people.

The work also depends on networks, electricity, funding and suppliers. Those dependencies connect a local choice to organisations and physical systems beyond the manager's direct control. Ignoring them does not make them somebody else's problem.

Asking good questions matters because it leads to evidence, action and follow-through. Questions alone cannot replace competent technical work, adequate resources or a process that lets somebody change a wrong decision. The manager's responsibility is to bring those parts together.

## Your turn, last time

Return to your decision record at the start of this chapter. Choose one sentence that is now more precise than your original claim. Explain what made it more precise: new evidence, a better definition, a distinction you had missed or a counterexample. You need not change your conclusion merely to demonstrate that you learned something.

Then revisit Chapter 9's investment reasoning. If you completed the optional company exercise, compare its two scenarios with the evidence available now. If you worked only with the fictional operator, revisit the additional evidence you wanted before approving more equipment. Has your list become more useful to a decision-maker?

You may not have a verdict. Record what remains unresolved and when you will look again. A good outcome does not prove that your reasoning was good, and a bad outcome does not by itself prove it was careless. Ask whether the choice was defensible given the information available and whether you responded properly when that information changed.

Now choose one action from your learning plan and give it a date. Name the decision you will observe, the claim you will check or the piece of work you will ask someone to challenge. “Develop better judgement” is an aspiration. A specific action gives you somewhere to begin.

Then the last question, which is the one Chapter 1 asked in a different form and which I want you to answer about yourself rather than about a company.

Imagine yourself in an organisation making a decision about technology. The decision will be made in a room. Some people in that room will understand the technology better; others will understand the business better. The group still needs to establish what the thing is actually for.

What do you need to become, starting now, to be the person in that room who asks?

Write it down. Keep it where you will find it.
