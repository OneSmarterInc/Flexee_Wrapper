# Introduction: How to use this book

An order is entered, but the goods do not arrive. A report shows an improvement, but customers are still unhappy. A new service looks inexpensive until someone counts the work needed to make it useful.

These are the kinds of situations you will examine in this book. You do not need a technical background to begin. You need to be willing to ask what is happening and follow the explanation beyond the screen.

Management information systems concerns how people use information and technology to organise work and make decisions. A system includes the people who enter and interpret records, the process they follow, the data they depend on and the technology they use. Understanding the connections helps you see why a change can succeed in one setting and disappoint in another.

As you read, keep three questions in view: What is the business trying to accomplish? How does the information system affect the work? What must a manager decide?

Use the diagrams and small tables to examine those connections. When a chapter gives you numbers, work through them. When it presents an inference, ask what evidence supports it. When it asks for a recommendation, explain both your choice and what might make you change it.

The book distinguishes reported events from fictional examples. A company story can teach a useful lesson without proving every explanation attached to it. An illustrative calculation makes its assumptions visible so you can inspect the reasoning. Pay attention to those distinctions; they are part of the learning.

Descriptions of operational tests concern authorised staff working under agreed conditions. For coursework, use the fictional cases or material your instructor has approved; you do not need to test a live system.

Keep the answer you write at the end of Chapter 1. You will return to it in the final chapter. The point is to see how your reasoning develops as you learn to connect technology with the work a business needs to do.
